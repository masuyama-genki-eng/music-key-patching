\section{Corpora, editions and splits \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~3.1)}}

\textbf{D-SYN} (Sec.~2.3): $200{,}000$ training, $10{,}000$ validation and $10{,}000$
test pieces from a functional-harmony generator, plus a separate $200{,}000/10{,}000$
pair for the reference model. The four sets share no piece: hashing every token
sequence gives zero overlap between train and test, train and reference-train, and
test and either reference set, so the reference model's ``data the other models never
see'' is disjoint by content and not merely by filename.

\textbf{Vocabulary and piece length.} The $124$ token types are $88$ absolute MIDI
pitches ($21$--$108$), $16$ beat positions, $16$ note lengths, one bar mark, and the
three structural tokens \texttt{PAD}, \texttt{BOS} and \texttt{EOS}. There is no key
and no chord symbol, and no token whose identity depends on a key. A piece runs
$12$--$22$ bars, median $17$, which is $278$--$508$ tokens, median $393$.

\textbf{Where transposition is and is not used.} Three different things could be
called transposition here, and only the last changes what a model sees at training
time.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Training augmentation (D-SYN only).} The R-Aug regime draws one uniform
transposition per sequence in token-id space at each step; R-NoAug draws none. The
corpus on disk is identical for both, so the six models differ in seed and in this
augmentation, not in data.
\item \emph{Estimating the targets.} The per-key means come from a key-balanced
sample. On Bach that sample is built by transposing each chorale through all twelve
tonics, because the $300$ chorales contain no F$\sharp$ major and no D$\sharp$ minor
at all and a direct mean would leave those two targets as zero vectors. The split is
by ORIGINAL chorale, so a piece's transpositions never straddle train and test.
\item \emph{The transposition reference.} Transposing the prompt itself is a
behavioural upper anchor, not a training or estimation step (Table~1).
\end{itemize}
No public checkpoint is fine-tuned, retrained, or augmented: we only read and edit
their activations.

\textbf{Bach chorales.} $300$ chorales in \texttt{**kern}, keys from the
Roman-numeral analyses of \emph{When in Rome}. A chorale is matched to its analysis by
number, never by order.

\textbf{POP909-CL.} $909$ files, $905$ load. Four are excluded by a rule fixed before
evaluation, with reasons recorded: two carry no key-signature event at all, and two are
listed by the release as having misaligned downbeats and algorithmic rather than expert
labels. $127$ pieces change key. Pieces split $0.7/0.15/0.15$ into train (probe,
per-key means, guard budget), search (layer and hyperparameter choices) and final
(touched once); a piece lands wholly in one part.

\textbf{Piece exclusions from a model's window.} Each tokenization bounds
representable time, and a piece is excluded when fewer than $34$ note positions fall
inside that bound. This removes exactly one piece (\texttt{247}, which opens with a
$70.5$-beat silence), for the REMI checkpoint only, in the train split only. The
held-out splits lose nothing.

\section{Probes, baselines and window settings \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~2.1, 4.1)}}
\label{sec:probes}

Multinomial logistic probe per layer, trained with AdamW; a two-layer MLP as a
secondary probe. Splits are by SEQUENCE, so no position of a piece appears in two
parts. The note-counting baselines (C3) look back over windows of $8$, $16$, $32$ and
$64$ pitch tokens and predict the key from the twelve pitch-class counts, by logistic
regression and by Krumhansl--Schmuckler. The control task (C1b) permutes the key
labels per sequence, giving the floor that is subtracted.

The pre-specified baseline peaked at the edge of its window sweep, so it was extended
with a wider, concatenated window, and the strongest of all of them
(\texttt{lr\_W16cat512}, $F_1$ $0.824$, against the pre-specified
\texttt{lr\_W64}'s $0.790$) is what the reported margin is measured against.

At layer $4$ the probe reaches macro-$F_1$ $0.927$ against that $0.824$; the control
floor is $0.032$, leaving $+0.071$ $[0.056,0.081]$. Against the pre-specified window
set alone the same layer gives $+0.105$ $[0.091,0.114]$, so widening the counter's
history costs margin without overturning the result. An untrained network of the same
shape reaches $0.243$, and the raw probe score is flat across layers $2$--$6$
($0.920$--$0.929$) while layer $1$ reaches $0.872$ and layer $0$ only $0.637$.

\textbf{The two layers nearest the input are exceptions.} Against the strongest
baseline the margin clears zero in layers $2$--$7$ of all six models (smallest lower
bound $+0.021$), but not at layer $0$ ($-0.231$) or layer $1$ ($+0.012$, CI
$[-0.00006, +0.021]$). Layer $1$ fails in three of the six models
(R-Aug\_s0, R-Aug\_s2, R-NoAug\_s1) and clears zero in the other three, so we treat
it as unresolved rather than positive. Against the pre-specified baseline alone layer
$1$ would clear zero in all six ($+0.044$ to $+0.062$) --- the layer-$1$ verdict is the
one conclusion in this paper that depends on which baseline is used.

\textbf{Seed replication.} All six main models support the probe result
independently against the strongest baseline, every confidence interval
excluding zero (peak layers are the same under either baseline):

\section{Quality gate and quality budget \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~3.2, 3.3)}}

\textbf{Gate} (before any intervention work): validation next-token top-$1$ must reach
$1.5\times$ the constant-predictor rate of $0.434$, and the in-key share of
unconditional generation must stay within $3$ standard deviations of the validation
mean. All $13$ finished checkpoints pass; the six main models measure
$0.878$--$0.881$.

\textbf{Budget} $\delta_{\text{PPL}}$, frozen before any edit is scored, as the
$90$th percentile of the reference model's perplexity rise across natural key changes:
$0.613$ over $7{,}989$ events on D-SYN. The public-model budgets are per corpus, since
a budget in one model's nats does not transfer: $0.849$ on Bach over $1{,}180$ events
(reference \texttt{music-medium-800k}) and $1.119$ on POP909 over $103$ events
(reference \texttt{music-large-800k}). A run whose frozen budget names a different
reference model is a hard error, not a warning.

\section{Two gates on the data \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~3.1, 3.2)}}

\textbf{Labels against notes.} Before any activation was read, the POP909 labels were
checked by estimating the key of every labelled segment of $\geq16$ notes and
comparing: thresholds \emph{exact} $\geq0.40$ and \emph{near} $\geq0.75$ were committed
first. Result over $1{,}060$ segments: exact $0.769$, near $0.958$; chance for exact is
$1/24=0.042$.

\textbf{Tokenizer against the model.} Each public model's token scheme is
re-implemented from its published configuration, so before probing we check that the
model finds correctly encoded music cheaper than corrupted music. The gate detects a
wrong field structure and a wrong time scale. It does NOT detect a uniform offset on
the note field, because in these vocabularies that is a transposition: the note codes
are instrument-major (verified against the reference implementation, which masks one
instrument as $128$ consecutive codes), so an off-by-one moves pitch and could reach
the instrument field only at pitch $127$, which never occurs in the $1.94$M notes used
here. That case is covered instead by two direct checks: all $15$ vocabulary constants
match the official implementation and the token streams agree on five real pieces of
$\approx600$ tokens; and the corpus conversion round-trips with every pitch and every
note preserved, onsets moving by at most one grid step ($5$\,ms absolute-time,
$25$\,ms beat-grid).

\textbf{Sampling.} The reference sampler masks the token families that do not belong in
the current slot and forbids control and special codes; ours draws from the whole
vocabulary, deliberately, so that no hand-written grammar can flatter the edit.
Measured on unedited continuations: not one generated note of $1{,}876$ left the
prompt's instrument block, and slot violations are $2.40\%$ on Bach and $0\%$ on pop,
all of them control or special codes, concentrated in one prompt of twelve.

\section{The key estimator: validated, and bounded \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~3.3)}}
\label{sec:estimator-ceiling}

\textbf{One estimate per continuation.} The estimator is applied once to the whole
continuation, not per token: the pitches are pooled into a single pitch-class
histogram --- $110$ notes at the median, truncated at the end-of-piece token and after
sixteen bars --- correlated against the twenty-four rotated profiles, and the argmax is
the continuation's key. Success is that key matching the installed one exactly, in both
tonic and mode; there is no partial credit for a neighbouring key.

This granularity is a deliberate choice with a cost, and the cost runs against us. A
continuation that needs a bar or two to settle into the installed key carries the
prompt's key in its histogram for those bars, and the pooled estimate is diluted by
them; a continuation that modulates part-way through gets one label rather than two.
The measure is therefore conservative about onset, which is part of why the tonic
metrics of \S\ref{sec:tonic} --- which read only how the music ends --- give much higher
numbers on the same continuations ($0.755$ against $0.355$). Where bar-level resolution
is what the question needs, we measure per bar instead: the persistence rows of
\S\ref{sec:oneshot} carry an estimate for each bar.

Our Krumhansl--Schmuckler implementation was checked against \texttt{music21}. The
Krumhansl--Kessler profiles are identical in both modes, and given the same pitch
content with uniform note lengths --- so that duration weighting and unweighted counts
coincide --- the two implementations return the SAME key on $800$ of $800$ real
segments, with no disagreement of any relation.

The same run measures how often the estimator recovers a key a human annotated, which
caps every absolute rate we report:

\section{Search stage and final test \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~3.3)}}

Layers and hyperparameters were chosen on a search stage that was allowed to look; the
numbers reported come from a final test on prompts no choice was ever made against.
For the public models: $20$ search prompts, $60$ held-out prompts, $12$ major targets,
$240$ new tokens, rank $24$, temperature $1.0$, top-$p$ $0.95$, seed $0$. MMT uses
$80$ steps instead of $240$, declared in advance, because its compound scheme emits one
position per note where the flat scheme emits three tokens.

Before any edit runs, a sham edit that projects out the subspace and re-inserts the
same component must reproduce the clean continuation token for token; it is computed
as a real edit rather than short-circuited, so a detached hook or a drifted position
makes it fail.

\textbf{Per-layer separation} (search stage, our model): the edit beats its control by
$0.030$ and $0.044$ at layers $0$ and $1$, then $0.193$, $0.258$, $0.303$, $0.259$,
$0.232$ and $0.228$ at layers $2$--$7$. The key is readable at layers $0$--$1$, where
writing to it barely works.

\textbf{Estimation condition.} Per-key targets come from a key-balanced sample, built
by transposing each piece through all twelve keys and splitting by original piece so
transpositions never straddle. On Bach this is forced: no F$\sharp$ major and no
D$\sharp$ minor occur, so a direct estimate leaves those two targets as zero vectors
and the F$\sharp$ major cell scores exactly $0$ --- edit $0.365$ with $11/12$ keys
separating for the $12$-layer model, and $0.439$ with $11/12$ for the $24$-layer one,
against $0.479$ and $0.557$ balanced.

\textbf{A prediction that failed.} The balanced re-estimation for AMT $\times$ pop was
specified in advance with its expected effect written down: ``$0.135$ becomes $\approx0.17$,
which likely still fails the per-key bar. The run happens either way, and its outcome
is reported either way.'' It reached $0.212$ with $9/12$ keys separating (the three
misses at $p_{\text{Holm}}$ $0.051$--$0.052$) and the in-key shares crossing. The
prediction was wrong, and the cell is no longer read as a failure.

\section{Install against steering, in full \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.6)}}

The pre-specified comparison replaces Eq.~(1)'s installation with an ADDITION of the
same value, so the only difference left is whether the old component $P_Vh$ is removed;
addition provably keeps it. Held-out prompts, paired by prompt against the install rows.

At matched displacement, replacement wins by $0.111$ ($95\%$ CI $[0.078,0.145]$, three
of twelve keys individually significant after correction), and the notes show the
mechanism: $28.8\%$ of the addition's continuations stay in the PROMPT's key, against
$11.5\%$ for the doubled addition and $0.6\%$ for the contrastive direction. Given
twice the displacement the addition overtakes replacement ($0.471$ against $0.355$),
which is the opposite of the pre-specified direction and is reported as it fell; it
pays for the extra push at $4\times$, where half the continuations break the quality
budget, and at $8\times$, where nearly all do. The difference-of-means direction leaves
the prompt key almost nowhere ($0.6\%$ of continuations, in-key share $0.438$), which
is the removal of the old component happening implicitly rather than by subtraction. In
minor keys the matched addition is far weaker still ($0.154$ against install's $0.495$,
all twelve keys significant).

So addition CAN set a key; what replacement buys is efficiency per unit of
perturbation, and what the comparison isolates is that removing what is already written
does part of the work.

\section{Is the installed value position-appropriate? \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.4)}}

The per-key means pool the token types --- of $144{,}000$ sampled positions, $62{,}509$
are pitch, $69{,}134$ bar or note length --- and the edit then writes the same
$P_V\mu_\kappa$ wherever the mask allows. That invites an objection to the position
result: editing only pitch positions may do nothing because the model does not consult
the key there, or because we wrote a value that does not belong there.

Estimating $\mu_\kappa$ from each family on its own separates the two. Inside $V$,
the two estimates of the SAME key agree to a cosine of $0.920$ on average
($0.877$--$0.953$) and are the same size (norm ratio $1.028$, $0.936$--$1.097$). For
scale, two DIFFERENT keys estimated from the same family agree only to $0.314$ on
average, at most $0.808$ --- so the worst-matched key across families is still more
aligned than any two distinct keys are within one. The value we write is therefore
close to position-independent, and the null result at pitch positions is not an
artefact of writing an alien target.

This settles the value; it does not settle the subspace. $V$ comes from a probe
trained on pooled positions too, so a position-specific $V$ remains a separate
question we did not test.

\section{Continuation length across tokenizations \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~3.2, 5)}}

count was declared for it when it was re-admitted, so it ran at the shared default.

edit --- the control condition has MORE short rows ($13.3\%$ against $10.0\%$ below

\section{Does the edit reach distant keys? \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.2)}}
\label{sec:distance}

A replacement that only worked between neighbouring keys would be a weak result: the
model might be nudged a step around the circle of fifths without any key being
installed. We therefore split every held-out row by the circle-of-fifths distance $d$
between the prompt's key and the installed key. Each of the $100$ prompts is evaluated
at all $11$ non-identity targets, so the rows are paired by construction: $200$ rows at
each of $d=1..5$ and $100$ at $d=6$, the tritone, where only one key lies.

Before reading the numbers we fixed what each outcome would mean. A confidence interval
on the distance coefficient that contains zero means the edit is distance-independent
and the claim is stronger than stated. A negative coefficient with $d=6$ still clearly
above the control means the effect is real but decays. No separation from the control at
$d=5$ and $6$ would mean the success rate is carried by near keys and the claim's reach
would have to be narrowed.

The first case holds, in both modes. Fitting success on $d$ with the prompt as a cluster
(GEE, exchangeable, logistic) gives $\hat\beta_d = -0.004$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.080, +0.072]$
for major prompts and $-0.046$, $[-0.116, +0.024]$ for minor. A mixed-effects fit and a
prompt-level cluster bootstrap over $2000$ replicates agree; all three are reported in
\texttt{results/reanalysis/a1\_a3/}. The tritone install succeeds at $0.340$ (major) and
$0.440$ (minor) against $0.000$ and $0.030$ for the matched-displacement control.

The control behaves in the opposite way, and this is the more informative half of the
figure. Its coefficient is strongly negative on major prompts, $-0.689$,
$[-0.937, -0.441]$: a random subspace succeeds at $0.195$ when the target happens to be
the neighbouring key and at $0.035$ or less everywhere beyond it. That is the profile of
an intervention that only ever registers as a success when the estimator was nearly going
to name the target anyway. The replacement does not have that profile.

We also asked whether the disturbance budget is what limits distant installs. It was
frozen from natural key changes, and natural key changes favour near keys, so a budget

each failure into "the estimator did not name the target", "the budget was exceeded", and
both, the budget-only failure rate on major prompts is flat in $d$ ($0.050$, $0.060$,
$0.035$, $0.050$, $0.065$, $0.080$). The hypothesis is not supported: failures are
dominated throughout by the estimator not naming the target ($0.405$ to $0.540$). On minor
prompts the budget-only rate does rise mildly with distance, from $0.115$ to $0.170$, and
we report that rather than the pooled figure.

\section{The geometry of the key subspace \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.4)}}
\label{sec:geometry}

$V$ is built from the probe's weights, so any structure inside it could be something the
probe imposed rather than something the model holds. The check is to measure the same
geometry on the raw class means $\mu_\kappa$, which the probe never touched.

Among major keys the circle of fifths is already there before any projection: the rank
correlation between cosine similarity and circle-of-fifths distance is $-0.936$ on the raw
means, $-0.961$ after removing the component common to all $24$ keys, and $-0.961$ after
projection into $V$. Projection sharpens the contrast --- neighbouring keys sit at $0.681$
and tritone-related keys at $-0.118$, against $0.978$ and $0.928$ before centring --- but
it does not create the ordering. Classical scaling of the centred means recovers the
circle of fifths as a closed ring (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp-geometry}). Relative keys are closer
than parallel ones ($0.581$ against $0.435$), which we report without a prediction, since
theory does not settle which should be nearer.

Two facts cut the other way and we state them plainly. Among minor keys there is
essentially no fifths ordering ($-0.063$ centred, $-0.216$ projected), so the structure
described above is a property of the major keys. And $V$ captures only $5.2\%$ of the
energy of $\mu_\kappa$ on average: it is a thin slice of the class means, not a summary of
them.

\section{A one-shot install, bar by bar \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.5)}}
\label{sec:oneshot}

The main text's edit is held for the whole continuation, which cannot distinguish a model
that carries the installed key forward from one that is simply being clamped. The search
stage also ran the write once, at the bar-$9$ boundary, and then left the model alone. Its
rows were never aggregated; we do that here.

Over the six bars the continuations reach, the one-shot install holds $0.687$ to $0.711$
of notes inside the installed key against $0.575$ to $0.584$ for the same write into a
random subspace --- separated at every bar by a prompt-paired Wilcoxon test after Holm
correction, with no visible decay. The sustained edit sits far above both, at $0.978$ to
$0.991$, which is the expected consequence of continuing to write.

Two limits belong with this result. The continuations only extend to bar $14$, so nothing
here speaks to longer horizons. More importantly, a persistent effect on the output does
not establish a persistent internal state: the model may be re-reading the key from the
notes it has already produced, which would reproduce this curve without anything being
held. Separating the two needs the token-splice control, which is outside this
submission's scope.

\section{Measuring the tonic, not the scale \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.2)}}
\label{sec:tonic}

that is blind to the distinction the introduction rests on. These metrics look at where
the music comes to rest instead. All $100$ continuations reach the end-of-piece token,
so the final chord is one the model composed rather than the point at which the token

Table~\ref{tab:supp-tonic} are floors on how often the music comes to rest in the

--- are the part to read.

\section{What else the edit changes \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.3)}}
\label{sec:selectivity}

The second success condition bounds how surprised a reference model is. That is a
summary, and an edit could stay inside it while thinning the texture or dragging the
register. Measured against the unedited continuation of the same prompt, the
replacement moves one attribute of three: notes per bar fall by $0.595$
($r=-0.41$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.2\times10^{-3}$), while mean pitch ($-0.092$, $p=0.43$)
and register width ($-0.223$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.36$) do not move at all.

The matched-displacement control moves all three, and much further: notes per bar by
$-3.471$ with a rank-biserial effect of $-1.000$, mean pitch by $-0.484$, register width
by $+1.323$. Comparing the two directly on the same prompts, the replacement's change is
the smaller on every attribute (Holm-corrected). A write of that size is not gentle in
general; this one is.

The third arm settles what a legitimate key change looks like on these measures.
Transposing the prompt into the target key and continuing with no edit at all moves the
mean pitch by $+5.648$ semitones --- a rank-biserial effect of $1.000$, since a
transposition must shift register --- while leaving notes per bar ($-0.057$, $p=0.92$)
and register width ($-0.096$, $p=0.52$) untouched. The two interventions therefore have
opposite footprints: transposition moves the register and not the texture, the edit
moves the texture a little and not the register. Neither is a subset of the other, and
neither resembles the matched control, which moves all three.

Three attributes the audit was designed to cover cannot be measured on this corpus, and
we report that rather than substituting something else: the vocabulary has no rest or
chord token, and every note carries duration $8$ by construction, so the duration
distribution is a point mass and its divergence is identically zero in every condition.

\section{Why minor scores higher \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.2)}}
\label{sec:minor}

Minor prompts reach $0.495$ against $0.355$. Both halves of the measurement favour
minor, and together they account for the gap.

The estimator is the larger half. The transposition reference is a continuation that is
in the target key by construction, so the rate it achieves is what this pipeline awards
a correct answer: $0.877$ in minor against $0.648$ in major. Read against its own mode's
ceiling, the edit reaches $0.564$ in minor and $0.548$ in major. The raw advantage of
$+0.139$ becomes $+0.015$. Essentially all of it is the estimator agreeing more readily
in minor, not the edit working better.

In-key note share is the smaller half. It allows minor the union of the natural,
harmonic and melodic forms --- nine of twelve pitch classes --- against seven for major,
so a minor continuation scores on notes that no single minor scale contains. Under the
harmonic minor the generator actually writes, the edited share falls from $0.957$ to
$0.903$, which is below major's $0.936$; the control falls from $0.764$ to $0.602$,
exactly major's $0.602$. The reported minor advantage on this measure is an artefact of
the definition, and the corrected comparison reverses its sign.

\section{Is the key subspace a print of the augmentation? \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~4.4, 5)}}
\label{sec:augmentation}

The class means carry the circle of fifths before any projection: the rank correlation
between cosine similarity and circle-of-fifths distance among the major keys is $-0.936$
raw and $-0.961$ once the component common to all $24$ keys is removed. The natural
objection is that transposition augmentation put it there, since a training set built by
transposing every piece into every key teaches the twelve keys as twelve versions of one
thing.

Three of the six trained models had no augmentation, so the objection is testable
without re-searching anything --- same layer, same construction of $V$, same statistic,
only the model changes. Augmented models give $-0.960 \pm 0.002$; models trained without
augmentation give $-0.963 \pm 0.003$. The two are indistinguishable, and every one of the
six falls between $-0.957$ and $-0.966$. The geometry is not a print of the augmentation.

This does not settle the wider version of the objection, and we separate them. That the
vocabulary contains no key token rules out the probe copying a label. It does not rule
out the training distribution making this representation close to inevitable, and on
synthetic data the key is literally the latent variable the generator samples from. The
public checkpoints are the part of the paper that speaks to that, and there the record is
incomplete: MMT's training procedure states a random pitch shift of $-5$ to $+6$
semitones, so MMT is augmented and its cell does not answer the objection; for the
Anticipatory checkpoints and for the REMI checkpoint we could not establish from public
information whether transposition augmentation was used, and we write that as unknown
rather than assume the common practice.

\section{Is the public comparison a common measurement? \normalfont\textit{(main Sec.~4.5)}}
\label{sec:public}

Table~2's cross-model rows are only meaningful if the note-counting baseline means the
same thing for each tokenization. It does. The window is counted in note events, not
tokens; all five checkpoints read the same $300$ chorales at the same $35{,}890$ sampled
positions; and the Krumhansl--Schmuckler baselines, which are a deterministic function of
the pitch-class histograms, come out bit-identical across every model. The fitted
baselines differ by at most $0.0060$, which is the noise of fitting the same inputs
twice. No recomputation was needed and none was done.

The value written by the edit is also insensitive to how it was estimated, in the cell
the question was raised about. Comparing the key-balanced and direct estimates key by
key, the smallest cosine is $0.9935$ for AMT on pop: the two are the same direction
everywhere, and the edit cannot tell them apart. On Bach the agreement is looser
($0.9744$ for MMT, $0.9281$ for REMI, $0.8593$ for AMT-36L), which is why the balanced
condition is the one reported there. Two Bach keys have a degenerate mean --- no position
in the corpus carries them --- and are excluded from the comparison rather than scored as
perfect agreement.

\section{Erasing the key, before any note \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~4.3, 5)}}
\label{sec:erase}

Every intervention so far writes a key in, which can only show that the subspace is
sufficient to set the key. Removing what it carries, and writing nothing back, asks
whether the model was using it. This is a forward pass at the bar-$9$ boundary, reading
the distribution over the next pitch; nothing is sampled.

Of that distribution, $0.9818$ sits on the prompt key's scale when the model is left
alone. Replacing the subspace with the grand mean of the $24$ class means drops it to
$0.9085$, and removing the component outright ($h - P_V h$) to $0.9069$; deleting an
equal-rank random subspace costs almost nothing, $0.9782$. The key removal costs about
twenty times what the random removal costs, and the effect is unanimous across the
hundred prompts (rank-biserial $-1.000$, $p = 3.9 \times 10^{-18}$, against $-0.396$
for the random control).

Two things keep this from being a demonstration of necessity, and both belong here.
The drop is decisive statistically and small musically: at $0.9069$ the model is still
overwhelmingly in the prompt's key, so the key is largely recoverable from outside $V$
or re-read from the prompt at each position. And the direction of the second measure
was not what we expected --- the entropy of the induced key posterior fell slightly
($1.936$ to $1.901$) rather than rising, and the most probable key was unchanged for
$0.71$ of prompts. Erasure loosens the model's commitment to the key's scale; it does
not leave the model unsure which key it is in.

\section{Does the edit reach a public model's first decision? \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~4.3, 4.5)}}
\label{sec:public-logits}

The main text shows on our own model that the edit changes the next-pitch distribution,
so it acts before any note is drawn. The same question can be put to a public
checkpoint without sampling. At the position the probe uses --- the step whose next
token is a note --- the distribution is folded over instruments to twelve pitch classes
and read as the log ratio of mass on the installed key's scale to mass on the prompt
key's.

On AMT small over Bach, at the layer the sweep had already chosen, that ratio moves from
$-0.0239$ unedited to $-0.0096$ under the edit (rank-biserial $0.494$,
$p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.0018$), while a rank-matched random subspace does not move it at
all ($-0.0216$, $r = 0.004$, $p = 0.98$). The mechanism demonstrated on the synthetic
model is present in a public checkpoint, and one limitation the paper carried is
answered.

The size is not comparable, and we do not present it as though it were. Our own model
separates the two keys by $0.695$ against $0.041$ for its control; here the separation
is $0.014$ of a log ratio. The claim this licenses is that the edit acts before
sampling in a public model too, not that it acts as strongly.

\section{What we did not run, and why \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~3.2, 4.5)}}

An earlier version of this section said AMT large's Bach edit had not been run,
because the guard would grade a $36$-layer model with a $24$-layer one. That reason
was wrong twice over. The requirement the freeze states is that the reference be a
different public checkpoint, not a larger one --- a model must not grade its own
output, and nothing about the argument needs the grader to be the bigger of the two.
And the assignment for all three targets was fixed in writing before any of them ran
(freeze §3: small$\rightarrow$medium, medium$\rightarrow$large, large$\rightarrow$medium),
so declining the run would have been a deviation from the pre-registration rather
than adherence to it. The run has since been made under that assignment and its cell
is reported in Table~2.

The balanced re-estimation was specified for AMT $\times$ pop only. Running it for MMT
and REMI after seeing AMT gain $+0.078$, on a split already spent, would be choosing an
analysis by its effect on a number; their cells keep the direct estimate, which is the
conservative direction for the one claim that uses them.

\section{Where this leaves the claims \normalfont\textit{(main Secs.~4, 5)}}

The sections above were written as separate answers to separate doubts. Read together
they move the paper in both directions, and a reader is owed the ledger rather than
the favourable half of it.

\textbf{Four findings weaken what the paper says.} The first is the most serious and
cannot be repaired by wording. Across training seeds the edit reaches $0.3555$,
$0.2182$ and $0.1927$ --- a standard deviation of $0.0876$ where we had pre-registered
$0.03$ as the bar for calling the effect independent of the training run, and the run
reported in the main text is the strongest of the three (\S\ref{sec:seeds}). The
second: minor's advantage over major is the measurement, not the edit. Read against
each mode's own transposition ceiling the gap falls from $+0.139$ to $+0.015$, and the
in-key definition that produced part of it reverses when minor is held to the seven
pitch classes the generator writes (\S\ref{sec:minor}). The third is a confound we
cannot close: the Bach cells were never length-matched, MMT hearing $240$ notes where
REMI hears $57$, and the re-score that would settle it needs continuations that were
not stored (\S\ref{sec:length}). The fourth is not new but is worth restating beside
the others --- the probe margin over note counting is $+0.105$, or $+0.071$ against
the strongest counter: real, and small.

\textbf{Against that, several objections do not survive contact with the data.} The
edit is distance-independent while its control is not (\S\ref{sec:distance}); failures
land beside the installed key rather than back at the prompt's (\S\ref{sec:landing});
the tonal centre moves and not merely the pitch collection, reaching about
three quarters of what a genuine transposition achieves on where the music comes to
rest (\S\ref{sec:tonic}); the finding holds under five key estimators, of which the
pre-registered one gives the lowest rate (\S\ref{sec:estimators}); the geometry and the
edit both survive training without augmentation (\S\ref{sec:augmentation},
\S\ref{sec:seeds}); the register and mean pitch are untouched (\S\ref{sec:selectivity});
resampling moves the rate by about a point (\S\ref{sec:gen-seed}); and the public
baseline is one measurement rather than three (\S\ref{sec:public}).

\textbf{Four things remain open, and none of them is closed by rewriting.} The Bach
length confound needs continuations we did not keep. The cadence detector is a
heuristic whose precision nobody has checked; its thirty saved detections are waiting
to be read. A one-shot edit persisting for six bars does not establish an internal
state, because the model may be re-reading the key from notes it has already produced,
and only the token-splice control separates those. And erasing the subspace costs
twenty times what an equal-rank random erasure costs but still leaves $0.9069$ of the
next-pitch mass in the prompt's key, so whatever the subspace carries, the key is
largely recoverable without it.

One boundary is worth stating because nothing in the paper tests it: every target is
one of the twelve keys of the prompt's own mode. No parallel-key install was attempted,
so nothing here shows that the mode can be changed at all.
